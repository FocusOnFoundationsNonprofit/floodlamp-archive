# FloodLAMP Archive Project - Target Audience
## Preamble
The audience profiles below describe who may find value in the archive and what kinds of materials are relevant. Use the accompanying combined summary file (`_archive-combined-metadata_summary_short.md`) to identify specific files and subcategories that match the user's stated interests. The manuscript (`floodlamp-manuscript-with-proposals.md`) synthesizes the archive into a single narrative and contains regulatory reform proposals not found elsewhere in the archive; it is referenced in relevant audience profiles below.

## Primary Audiences

### 1. Pandemic Preparedness Researchers and Policy Analysts
__Who they are:__ Academics, think tank researchers, government policy staff (FDA, CDC, ASPR, BARDA, RADx, ITAP, NSC, OSTP), and international health organizations studying lessons from COVID-19.

__What they would use in the FloodLAMP Archive:__
- The manuscript (`floodlamp-manuscript-with-proposals.md`) — synthesizes pilot outcomes, the FDA regulatory experience, and presents regulatory reform proposals including a registered screening program pathway, a dedicated pandemic testing preparedness authority, open protocol authorization mechanisms, and AI-enabled FDA reform. This is the single most important file for this audience.
- Regulatory correspondence and FDA submission packages as case studies in EUA navigation.
- Surveillance framing documents as precedent for non-diagnostic testing frameworks.
- Pilot data as evidence for decentralized testing efficacy.
- Open EUA Consortium documents for analysis of "generic diagnostics" regulatory models.

__Why it may be relevant now:__ Post-pandemic reports consistently identify testing infrastructure as a critical gap. This archive provides primary-source operational documentation of an alternative approach that was attempted and validated, including what worked, what didn't, and why. The manuscript's reform proposals are grounded in the documented experience and offer specific, actionable recommendations.

### 2. Health Policy Researchers Focused on Emergency Authorities and Regulatory Issues
__Who they are:__
Policy researchers, administrative law scholars, public health law practitioners, and government affairs teams studying how emergency authorities, enforcement discretion, and communication rules shaped pandemic-era testing—often with an interest in reform options.

__What they would use in the FloodLAMP Archive:__
- The manuscript (`floodlamp-manuscript-with-proposals.md`) — contains specific reform proposals for a formalized screening vs. diagnosis distinction, a registered screening program pathway with permissionless entry and accountability, and analysis of the structural gap between surveillance and diagnostic testing categories. The manuscript’s discussion of the “incentive problem” in pandemic testing and its proposal for a dedicated pandemic testing preparedness authority are directly relevant to policy reform.
- Primary-source documentation of CMS enforcement discretion and related constraints that shaped non-diagnostic screening programs.
- The archive’s description of the “language game” problem (how programs could refer participants for confirmatory testing while being restricted in plain-language result communication), supported by the underlying templates and correspondence.
- Proposal and concept materials that outline what a lighter-weight pathway might have looked like (presented as historical artifacts, not as current policy).

__Why it may be relevant now:__
- Post-emergency evaluations frequently focus on the gap between what was needed operationally and what existing regulatory categories easily supported. The archive may assist researchers who want grounded examples rather than retrospective generalizations.
- Because the archive includes both “what was attempted” and “what did not advance,” it can support analysis of why certain governance ideas stall (e.g., unclear authority, institutional risk tolerance, or inconsistent enforcement).
- The manuscript’s reform proposals are grounded in documented operational experience and offer specific, actionable recommendations rather than abstract calls for reform.

### 3. Public Health Surveillance/Screening Program Designers and Operators
__Who they are:__
Local and state public health agencies, municipal health offices, school district health coordinators, epidemiology teams, public health labs, workplace safety officers, emergency management professionals, and preparedness units tasked with designing or evaluating community surveillance/screening programs (especially those that rely on serial testing, rapid turnaround, or non-traditional testing sites).

__What they would use in the FloodLAMP Archive:__
- The Pilots collection: program documentation for 11 surveillance testing programs across four states, including site configurations (e.g., schools, municipal workforces), operational timelines, and observed positivity patterns.
- The cleaned, standardized pilot dataset (participant tests, positive tubes, referral confirmation rates, etc.) suitable for retrospective analysis and benchmarking.
- Site checklists, facilities requirements, and communications guidelines for standing up surveillance-style testing programs.
- Materials describing the household/family pooling model (including the California preschool family pooled screening pilot whitepaper) as a specific configuration option rather than a generic "pooling" concept.
- Guides and SOPs that describe turnaround targets, staffing roles, training expectations, and practical constraints (e.g., what can realistically be done by non-lab operators).
- Family information flyers, consent templates, result interpretation charts, and referral notification workflows.

__Why it may be relevant now:__
- Many jurisdictions continue to debate what "sustainable surveillance" should look like outside emergency conditions (sentinel approaches, triggers for escalation, how to balance cost vs. timeliness). The archive provides a concrete example set—what was tried, with what operational tradeoffs, and what outputs were measured.
- For readers considering distributed models, the archive's emphasis on program configuration (pooling design, deconvolution workflows, referral communications) may be more informative than assay performance alone, particularly when the primary objective is early warning and continuity planning rather than individual clinical diagnosis.
- These materials were templated and tested in real-world conditions across schools, municipal workforces, and emergency response organizations, and may serve as a starting point for future program design.

### 4. Diagnostic Test Developers and Clinical Laboratory Scientists (LAMP/Isothermal Focus)
__Who they are:__ Companies and academic labs developing isothermal amplification tests, high-complexity CLIA lab directors, assay development scientists, translational research groups, and diagnostics teams evaluating LAMP or LAMP-adjacent workflows—particularly those pursuing low-cost, decentralized, screening-oriented, or point-of-care applications where equipment cost and throughput matter.

__What they would use in the FloodLAMP Archive:__
- Primer sequences and preparation protocols.
- Extraction-free workflow documentation (TCEP/EDTA inactivation).
- FDA EUA clinical validation materials for the QuickColor COVID-19 Test, including comparator testing context, PPA/NPA, limit of detection, gene targets, and cross-reactivity/interference claims as documented at the time.
- Technical background documents on assay optimization choices and screening-oriented reasoning (e.g., sensitivity tradeoffs and serial testing logic).
- Validation guide and clinical evaluation protocols.
- FDA submission packages as templates.
- Contamination control procedures specific to LAMP.
- Operational evidence from pilots linking assay performance to real-world workflows (training, throughput constraints, sample handling, deconvolution).

__Why it may be relevant now:__
- LAMP technology continues to develop for various pathogens. Even when the target pathogen or platform of interest differs, the archive provides a full-stack view of what it looked like to take a molecular screening concept from analytical characterization through clinical comparison to field deployment.
- FloodLAMP's operational learnings around contamination control, result interpretation, and field deployment address practical challenges not typically covered in academic publications.
- The archive is explicit about limitations: the test was not FDA-authorized, and the clinical evaluation showed performance characteristics that may or may not meet a given reader's requirements. That transparency may be useful for comparative study rather than replication.

### 5. Regulatory Affairs, Quality Systems, and Diagnostics Counsel
__Who they are:__
Regulatory affairs professionals at diagnostics companies, regulatory consultants, laboratory directors considering LDT development, compliance leaders in health systems, clinical lab counsel, quality systems teams, and healthcare attorneys who need primary-source examples of EUA interactions, surveillance frameworks, and the practical consequences of ambiguous regulatory categories.

__What they would use in the FloodLAMP Archive:__
- The Regulatory collection: FDA submissions and correspondence (pre-EUA, EUA, deprioritization, interactive review), guidance documents, and records of how particular interpretations were communicated and enforced in practice.
- CMS surveillance testing guidance and FloodLAMP's interpretation of its constraints.
- Templated materials for operating under surveillance framing: consent forms, participant communications guidelines, liability waivers, and documented constraints on terminology.
- LDT regulatory landscape documents (including 2024 ACLA litigation materials, though this is a tiny selection of material related to FDA/LDT issues during the pandemic).
- IRB protocol and informed consent templates.
- The archive's record of FDA town hall discussions (including the AI-assisted retrieval demo) to locate what regulators said publicly at different points in the pandemic and how developers interpreted that guidance.

__Why it may be relevant now:__
- The CLIA/FDA boundary remains a live topic in U.S. diagnostics regulation. The FDA issued an LDT final rule on May 6, 2024; it was vacated by a federal district court on March 31, 2025; and the FDA subsequently reverted the regulation to its pre-2024 text via a final rule published September 19, 2025.
- Against that backdrop, the archive's "regulatory surveillance pathway" materials may be relevant as a documented case study of how teams navigated (and sometimes struggled with) surveillance vs. diagnostic framing, especially when programs were operationally communicating individual-level information while being constrained in language.
- For healthcare attorneys and health law educators, the surveillance consent templates, liability waivers, and FDA warning correspondence provide case materials illustrating the intersection of regulatory compliance, communications constraints, and operational risk.
- The archive may also inform discussions about whether a lighter-weight regulatory framework is needed — one that is permissionless but carries obligations for registration and reporting, so that organizations can stand up screening programs quickly when the need arises, without the legal friction and multi-stakeholder negotiations that the current framework required even deep into the pandemic.

### 6. Global Health Implementers and Decentralized Molecular Capacity Planners
__Who they are:__
Global health NGOs, ministries of health, international development organizations, and partners supporting decentralized laboratory capacity and community surveillance—particularly in contexts where central lab turnaround is unreliable, workforce and equipment budgets are constrained, or diagnostic access in resource-limited settings is a primary concern.

__What they would use in the FloodLAMP Archive:__

- Training materials and SOPs designed for non-laboratory operators (adaptable to different credentialing environments, with appropriate local review).
- Program configuration concepts, especially household pooling, when the objective is stopping spread in a group/organization through rather than purely individual clinical diagnosis.
- Supply chain documentation for off-the-shelf components and open licensing frameworks relevant to technology transfer.

__Why it may be relevant now:__

FloodLAMP's approach of maximum openness, component disclosure, and simplified operations was designed for adaptation beyond U.S. CLIA laboratories.
- The archive can be read as a case study with documented constraints and incomplete pathways (including regulatory dependencies that differ internationally), which may help readers identify what would need to change in their own contexts.

### 7. Critical Workforce and Emergency Response Organizations
__Who they are:__ Operational leaders and occupational health teams in critical services (fire/EMS, police, utilities, municipal departments), emergency management coordinators, and organizations in high-consequence settings where staffing continuity is a primary objective during surges—particularly those who participated in FloodLAMP pilots or similar programs.

__What they would use in the FloodLAMP Archive:__

- "Bring-up" and remote enablement documentation relevant to creating local molecular testing capacity without relying on a central lab for every step, including mobile lab configuration guides.
- Training and certification materials designed for non-laboratory operators in operational settings.
- Communications templates and referral workflows designed for surveillance framing rather than clinical diagnosis.

__Why it may be relevant now:__
- First responders and essential workers were critical infrastructure during COVID-19 and remain high-priority populations for future outbreak response. Continuity planning discussions often return to the same operational constraints seen during the pandemic: delays in centralized testing during surges, staffing shortages, and the need for rapid decision support even when clinical-grade pathways are not feasible.
- The archive documents one implemented approach to decentralized molecular testing in fire departments and other operational settings, including metrics, operational lessons, and associated regulatory and communication constraints. A reader may use these materials as a comparison point when evaluating alternatives such as vendor-run testing, point-of-care antigen strategies, or internal lab partnerships.

### 8. Open Protocol Diagnostics, Open Science, and Regulatory Reform Advocates
__Who they are:__
Academic labs, open-source biotech communities, standards-oriented diagnostics groups, researchers and advocates for open-access diagnostics and pandemic preparedness reform, some reagent/platform manufacturers interested in shared validation components and reducing duplicative validation work — especially under emergency conditions, and advocates for alternative models for medical device regulation.

__What they would use in the FloodLAMP Archive:__
- The manuscript (`floodlamp-manuscript-with-proposals.md`) — presents the open-source EUA model in the context of a peer-reviewed paper, including the SalivaDirect precedent, FloodLAMP's extension with a blanket Right of Reference, the “generics of diagnostics” framework, the stalled Open EUA Consortium, and proposals for institutionalizing open protocol authorization as a standard regulatory pathway.
- Open EUA Consortium vision documents and materials describing FloodLAMP's proposed rights-of-reference approaches and primer standardization discussions.
- Letters from FloodLAMP to FDA, and records of engagement during FDA Townhall sessions on Right of Reference—including public statements about potential "synergy" from shared validation.
- Anne Wyllie (SalivaDirect) collaboration materials, regulatory innovation award nomination letter, and references to SalivaDirect's designation model as an existence proof for open protocol scaling.
- Proposals articulating the case for decentralized, open, low-cost testing as an alternative to individually validated proprietary assays.

__Why it may be relevant now:__
- Open protocol EUA limited to only SalivaDirect and not expanded during the pandemic. Post-pandemic debates about diagnostic access, regulatory flexibility, and shared validation frameworks continue, but few concrete case studies document what was actually proposed and attempted.
- Addresses whether "open protocol" approaches can coexist with regulatory expectations and quality controls, with the archive including specific artifacts: what was proposed, what questions emerged from regulators and collaborators, and where progress slowed or stalled.
